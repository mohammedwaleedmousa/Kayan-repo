The Economic Architecture
How Kayan prices, what each engagement actually costs, what it sells as a product, how money physically moves, and how revenue is retained
Session 1 defined what Kayan is. This document defines whether it makes money. It corrects the take-rate assumption on the basis of comparable-market evidence, replaces a flat percentage with a risk-priced fee architecture, gives the cost equation that governs every quotation, productises what Kayan sells, and specifies how money physically moves given Yemen&apos;s banking reality.

1. The correction
Session 1 carried forward a 20 per cent take rate from the historical estate without testing it. That number is wrong, and it is wrong in the direction that would have quietly killed the company. [JUDGEMENT — this is a self-correction]
Comparable managed-delivery businesses do not charge 20 per cent. They charge roughly double that, and they carry less risk than Kayan proposes to carry.

1.1 What the comparison actually establishes
The fee is not a percentage of a transaction. It is the price of the risk transferred. Braintrust carries almost no risk and charges 10 to 15 per cent. Andela and Turing carry matching, management and continuity risk and take around half the invoice. Kayan proposes to carry more than either — identity risk, competence risk, contract risk, payment risk and delivery accountability, in an operating environment where power and connectivity are unreliable.
A single flat rate cannot express that. So Kayan does not have a take rate. It has a fee architecture in which the fee is a function of the risk the client hands over. [DECISION D-37]

2.1 Why the tiers also protect the talent
A higher tier does not mean the contributor earns less. It means Kayan earns more for carrying more. Contributor rates are set by role, tier of the professional and the specialisation — they do not vary with what Kayan charges the client. This must be true, and must be seen to be true, or the disclosure principle collapses and with it the trust asset. [RULE]
3. The Kayan Price Equation
No quotation is issued without running this. It is deliberately built as a floor plus a target, because the commonest way a services company dies is by discounting below a floor it never calculated.
3.1 The floor
F = D + S + Q + T + W + O
Term
What it is
How it is estimated
D — Direct delivery
Contributor fees for every pod role, at agreed rates for the hours scoped
Role × rate × scoped hours, summed
S — Supervision
Pod lead time plus Kayan delivery management time
A percentage of D, set by tier: lower at S1, higher at S3
Q — Quality assurance
Independent review before submission
Reviewer hours; zero at S1, mandatory at S2 and S3
T — Transaction
Payment rails in and out, foreign exchange spread, escrow administration
A percentage of gross flow, set from observed banking cost, not assumed
W — Warranty and rework reserve
Held against revisions and post-acceptance fixes
A percentage of D, set by tier and by scope novelty
O — Overhead allocation
Hub, platform, verification, administration, insurance
Allocated per engagement on a stated basis, reviewed quarterly
Rule: no engagement is ever priced below F. A price below F is not a discount, it is a loss, and it requires named executive approval and is recorded as a business-development investment rather than as a sale. [RULE — this single rule prevents the commonest failure in project services]

Target price  =  F ÷ (1 − π)      where π is the target contribution margin for the tier
Then four adjustments, each a multiplier applied to the target, each recorded on the quotation so that the reason for the price is auditable:

The 1.05 factor on the floor exists so that even a fully subsidised engagement contributes something rather than consuming capacity for nothing.
3.3 The disclosed-fee discipline
Kayan discloses its fee to both sides. This is unusual, it is a genuine competitive risk, and it is correct — a company whose product is trust cannot conceal its own economics.
It is survivable only because the fee is justified by what is promised rather than by what is hidden. A client who sees 45 per cent and also sees a fixed price, a guaranteed date, free contributor replacement and a warranty is buying an insurance product and can evaluate it as one. A client who sees 45 per cent with no stated promise attached will refuse, correctly. Therefore: the fee is never quoted without the tier, and the tier is never quoted without its promises in writing. [RULE]

4.1 The concentration rule
Segment A will dominate early revenue because it is the only segment with budget certainty. That is correct commercially and dangerous structurally. The rule: when any single client exceeds a stated share of trailing twelve-month revenue, a written mitigation plan is required, and when the top three clients exceed a stated share, new segment A pursuit is paused until segments B and C are grown. Thresholds are set in the financial model. [RULE — Constitution R9]

5.1 The rules that make a Ready Product work
Every product has an explicit exclusion list. What is not included is stated more prominently than what is, because scope creep is where fixed-price products lose money.
Every product is offered at S2 by default, with S3 available at the stated multiplier. S1 is not offered on Ready Products — the whole point is that Kayan manages them.
Every product carries a defined revision allowance. Revisions beyond it are a change request with a price, not a favour.
Every product has a target contribution margin and is reviewed monthly against actual. A product that misses its margin twice in a quarter is repriced or retired.
Every product is delivered by the same pod shape, so that the cost estimate improves with every delivery rather than being re-guessed.
The catalogue is the mechanism by which Kayan stops selling hours and starts selling outcomes. It is also the only realistic route to serving segments C and D at all, because a bespoke process cannot be justified at those price points. [JUDGEMENT]

8. Treasury architecture — the constraint Session 1 understated
Research conducted for this document establishes that the payment risk is worse and more structural than the Constitution stated. It is not a matter of choosing a bank.
Finding
Evidence
Consequence for Kayan
Correspondent banking has been severed
Sanctions and anti-money-laundering pressure led many international banks to cut correspondent relationships with Yemeni institutions from the early 2010s onward [RESEARCH CLAIM]
An international client cannot reliably wire funds into a Yemeni account. The collection rail cannot sit inside Yemen.
The central bank is split
Two rival central banks since 2016, with parallel monetary and regulatory systems, dual exchange rates and inconsistent regulation [RESEARCH CLAIM]
Exchange rate and regulatory treatment are not single-valued. Every contract must state which rate applies and when it is fixed.
European banks remain reluctant
Reported continued refusal of European banks to interact with Yemeni banks [RESEARCH CLAIM]
European clients are the hardest corridor. Do not build the launch pipeline on them.
Informal channels dominate
Reliance on hawala and informal networks persists because formal channels are slow, costly or inaccessible [RESEARCH CLAIM]
Payouts inside Yemen will partly rely on non-bank rails. These must be documented and controlled, not pretended away.
8.1 The two-rail design
The architecture that follows from the evidence is a separation of collection from distribution. [DECISION D-39 — the single most consequential structural decision in this document]
Rail
Function
Location
What it must do
Rail A — Collection and escrow
Receive client funds; hold them against milestones; release on acceptance
A jurisdiction with functioning correspondent banking. Candidates to be assessed: UAE, Oman, Jordan, Egypt, Türkiye.

Tadamon Islamic International Bank, Aden, plus controlled non-bank rails for those without accounts

Move value from Rail A to Rail B
The structural question this document cannot answer alone
Must be lawful, documented, sanctions-compliant, and repeatable at volume
The bridge is the open item. Three routes exist in principle — an affiliated entity in the collection jurisdiction, a licensed payment service provider, or a contracted exchange and settlement arrangement — and each has legal, tax and compliance consequences that require counsel. What is settled is that pretending a single Aden account can serve both functions is not viable, and that this must be resolved before the launch date, not after. [OPEN — Q-11]
8.2 The sequencing insight this produces
There is a route that makes launch possible before the bridge is fully solved, and it changes the go-to-market order.
Clients who already operate inside Yemen — international agencies with Yemen offices, INGOs, local institutions — can pay a Yemeni vendor through mechanisms they already run. For these clients, Rail A is not required.
Diaspora clients can pay through established remittance corridors into Yemen, which are functioning even where formal banking is not.
GCC and international corporate clients with no Yemen presence are the segment that genuinely requires Rail A.
Therefore the launch pipeline is built from segments A, E and C, in that order, and segment B is pursued in earnest only once Rail A exists. This is not a retreat from ambition. It is the recognition that a payment failure on a first engagement with a GCC client would cost more in reputation than the engagement is worth. [DECISION D-40]
9. The Andela warning
Andela was founded on a model materially identical to Kayan Forge: recruit talent in an underserved market, train them intensively, place them with international clients. In 2020 it abandoned that model, moving from campus-based training to global remote sourcing, explicitly to cut capital expenditure and widen the talent pool. Training programmes were cut and staff were laid off in the transition. [RESEARCH CLAIM — verify before external use]
This is the most important comparable in the entire research set, and it must be confronted rather than ignored.

Why Kayan&apos;s structure differs
What must remain true
Training was funded from company capital, making each cohort a large upfront cost against uncertain placement
Forge is sponsor-funded. The cohort is paid for by a third party who values the outcome independently of whether Kayan places the graduate.
If Forge ever becomes company-funded at scale, this comparison becomes a prediction. Sponsor funding is not a nice-to-have; it is the structural difference.
Placement demand was narrow — senior software engineers for technology companies

10. Positioning correction: the category already exists
Session 1 proposed frontier economy as an owned category. Research shows that a closely adjacent category is already established — impact sourcing — with a recognised standard, an industry coalition, dedicated funder attention and existing procurement pathways. [RESEARCH CLAIM]
Inventing a parallel category next to an established one is a strategic error. It forfeits the procurement doors, the funder familiarity and the standards infrastructure that already exist, in exchange for a phrase nobody is searching for. [JUDGEMENT — this reverses part of the Session 1 position]
The correct move is to enter the established category and then differentiate sharply within it:
Established impact sourcing
Kayan&apos;s position within it
Predominantly task and process work: data annotation, digitisation, transcription, content moderation
Professional work: design, engineering, analysis, translation, research, finance, evaluation
Workers are employed to perform tasks under supervision
Professionals are verified, hold a portable record, and progress through a published ladder
Impact is measured as jobs created
Impact is measured as absorption into recorded, paid, contracted work, with a defined ratio
Criticised for low pay and limited progression
Disclosed fees, rates independent of client price, and a visible progression route from apprentice to assessor
Concentrated in relatively stable low-income markets
Operates in an active conflict economy, which is precisely the harder claim and the more defensible one
The sentence becomes: Kayan is impact sourcing for professional work, built in the hardest operating environment in the world. That sits inside a category funders already fund and procurement teams already recognise, while claiming the part of it nobody has taken. [DECISION D-41]
Practical consequence: assess the recognised impact sourcing standard and the industry coalition as a certification and membership route, and treat B Corporation certification as a medium-term objective. These are procurement keys, not badges. [RECOMMENDATION]
